Retinol or retinaldehyde?

Skin converts retinoids to retinoic acid, the form that acts on skin cells. Retinol needs two oxidation steps to get there; retinaldehyde (retinal) needs one. Retinal therefore starts closer to the active form, while retinol is the most widely used and recognised name on pack. Both are sensitive to light and oxygen, which is why we focus on encapsulated grades.

Formulating with retinoids

  • Add encapsulated retinoids in the cool-down phase, below the temperature on the TDS.
  • Minimise light and air: work under yellow light and use nitrogen where the TDS calls for it (RetinEZ is the exception).
  • Pack in airless pumps or laminate tubes.
  • Check the retinoid level you claim against the grade's loading; for example, 0.5% Microsponge® 520RA ≈ 0.1% retinal.

Frequently asked questions

Is retinaldehyde better than retinol?

Retinaldehyde is one conversion step closer to retinoic acid. Which works better in a product depends on dose, formula and stability.

Why use encapsulated retinol?

Encapsulation protects retinol from light and oxygen and releases it gradually, supporting stability and lower irritation.

Do you have BHT-free retinol?

Yes. Microsponge® N 720TRE, PolyPore® options and some HBS Retinol 50 versions are BHA- and BHT-free.
