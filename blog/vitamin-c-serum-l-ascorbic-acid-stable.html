Pure vitamin C is the best-studied antioxidant in skin care, and the hardest to keep stable. Here is how protected L-ascorbic acid solves the yellowing problem, with supplier data and formulation advice.

A vitamin C serum is only as good as the vitamin C inside it on the day it is used. L-ascorbic acid, the pure form of vitamin C, is the most studied antioxidant in skin care, but it oxidises quickly in water, and that is why so many serums turn yellow, then orange, within weeks. Most suppliers solve this by switching to a derivative such as ethyl ascorbic acid or magnesium ascorbyl phosphate. Kalptaru Naturals takes a different route: we supply real L-ascorbic acid in protected forms, so formulators in India can make a stable vitamin C serum without giving up the molecule the science is built on.

This guide explains why pure vitamin C breaks down, how encapsulation protects it, what the supplier data shows, and how to formulate with SCH ShellCare Vit C, SCH Actives Antiox C and Microsponge vitamin C.

What makes a good vitamin C serum? Start with pure L-ascorbic acid

L-ascorbic acid is the biologically active form of vitamin C. In skin it donates electrons to neutralise free radicals produced by UV light and pollution, it is a cofactor for the enzymes that build collagen, and it reduces melanin formation by interfering with tyrosinase. A peer-reviewed review of topical vitamin C summarises these mechanisms and notes that most of the clinical evidence for vitamin C in skin care comes from L-ascorbic acid itself.

The classic absorption work by Pinnell and colleagues showed that L-ascorbic acid entered skin only when the formula pH was below about 3.5, and that tissue levels rose with concentration up to about 20%. Hence the familiar "15–20% vitamin C at low pH" serum: effective, but acidic, sometimes stinging and chemically fragile.

Why brand owners still ask for "real" vitamin C

  • Label appeal: informed Indian consumers look for "L-ascorbic acid" on the INCI list.
  • No conversion step: derivatives must be converted to ascorbic acid in skin before they act; pure vitamin C is already active.
  • Evidence: the best-known studies, including the vitamin C + E + ferulic acid work, were done with L-ascorbic acid.

Why vitamin C serum turns yellow or orange

In water, ascorbic acid is oxidised to dehydroascorbic acid (DHA). That step is reversible, but dehydroascorbic acid then breaks down irreversibly into compounds that brown, the same chemistry that browns cut fruit. Light, heat, dissolved oxygen, higher pH and trace metals such as iron and copper all speed it up. The colour change is the visible sign that active vitamin C is being lost, and it is the most common complaint in reviews of vitamin C serums.

The SCH study report describes the same problem: pure ascorbic acid degrades quickly with light, heat and air, losing efficacy and discolouring.

How encapsulation keeps vitamin C stable

Encapsulation puts a physical barrier between ascorbic acid and the things that destroy it: oxygen, light, water activity and reactive ingredients in the rest of the formula. The active is released on skin, where it is needed, instead of reacting in the bottle.

The three technologies we supply use different barriers:

  • Polymer capsules (SCH ShellCare Vit C): a biodegradable polycaprolactone shell around the ascorbic acid.
  • Gold-particle carrier (SCH Actives Antiox C): ascorbic acid and ferulic acid carried on gold particles of about 170 nm.
  • Porous microspheres (Microsponge vitamin C): ascorbic acid loaded into the pores of acrylate or cellulose microspheres.

SCH ShellCare Vit C from Special Chemicals (SCH), Barcelona, is L-ascorbic acid encapsulated in polycaprolactone (PCL) capsules. It is part of SCH's ShellCare family of encapsulated actives, and it is the most direct answer to the question "how do I keep pure vitamin C from turning yellow?" Why polycaprolactone?

PCL is a biodegradable, biocompatible polymer already used in medical and cosmetic applications. According to SCH's ShellCare technology brochure, it degrades slowly in the epidermis through hydrolysis of its ester bonds, which makes it suited to prolonged release of the active. The shell shields the vitamin C from light, air and heat until application, and because the skin meets the capsule rather than free acid, SCH also positions encapsulation as gentler on skin. SCH's penetration study with a fluorescent marker in PCL capsules showed more of the marker inside skin and in the receptor fluid after 20 hours than the free marker.

Does protected vitamin C stay stable? What the data shows

SCH tested SCH Actives Antiox C against a free mixture of vitamin C and ferulic acid in water and in an emulsion, stored for 30 weeks at 45 °C and measured by HPLC. The free mixture collapsed within weeks: the report states it kept only 16% of its vitamin C at week 5 in water (27% in the emulsion) and was essentially gone by week 20 to 30. Antiox C kept 97–99% throughout, in both product types.

Antioxidant activity followed the same pattern. In an ABTS radical-scavenging test, 0.3% Antiox C reached 89.8% scavenging at 40 hours against 55.9% for the free mixture at the same vitamin C to ferulic acid ratio.

Skin-cell and clinical results

In UVA-exposed human dermal fibroblasts, pre-treatment with 0.3% Antiox C gave 185% cell viability relative to the UVA-only group, compared with 153% for 15% vitamin C and 131% for 0.5% ferulic acid. Caspase-3, a marker of cell death, was reduced most by Antiox C.

The report also gives these results for 0.3% Antiox C: a 69% vitamin C release through pig skin in Franz cells after 20 hours, a 2.6-fold increase in collagen I (COL1A1) expression in fibroblasts, a 44% reduction in tyrosinase activity, and, in 20 volunteers, 81% of skin antioxidant capacity preserved after ozone exposure versus 45% with placebo. After 28 days of twice-daily use, firmness rose 37% and elasticity 39% (Cutometer, 20 participants), with an irritation index of 0.

These are supplier studies on the ingredient; a finished serum still needs its own testing.

How to formulate a stable vitamin C serum

Order of addition and temperature

  • Build the base first (water phase, humectants, thickener, any emulsion) and cool it.
  • Add the protected vitamin C in the cool-down phase. SCH recommends below 40 °C for ShellCare Vit C; treat all three technologies the same way.
  • Mix gently. High shear after addition can damage capsules and microspheres.

pH, chelation and compatibility

  • Keep the finished pH within the range the ingredient is designed for; ShellCare Vit C itself is pH 3.5–6.5. Check the TDS for each grade before adjusting.
  • Add a chelating agent to bind trace iron and copper, which catalyse oxidation.
  • Pair with ferulic acid and vitamin E for extra antioxidant protection, as published work supports.

Packaging

  • Use opaque, airless or pump packaging to limit light and oxygen.
  • Avoid large headspace and clear dropper bottles for open-market products.

Formulating vitamin C serums for India

India is a hard market for vitamin C. Storage and transport often run above 35–40 °C, humidity is high for much of the year, and a serum may sit on a shop shelf or in a courier van for weeks. Free L-ascorbic acid that seems fine in an air-conditioned lab can yellow by the time it reaches the customer. That is why the 45 °C data above matters.

  • Run accelerated stability at 40–45 °C, not only room temperature, and track colour as well as vitamin C assay.
  • Plan for daytime use: Indian consumers often use vitamin C in the morning, so advise pairing with sunscreen.
  • Keep claims proportionate. Brightening and antioxidant claims must be backed by tests on the finished product, and labels must follow Indian cosmetic rules.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my vitamin C serum turn yellow?

Free L-ascorbic acid oxidises in water to dehydroascorbic acid, which breaks down further into coloured compounds. Light, heat, oxygen, higher pH and trace metals speed this up. Encapsulated or carried vitamin C, chelating agents and airless, opaque packaging slow it down.

Is L-ascorbic acid better than ethyl ascorbic acid?

L-ascorbic acid is the active form of vitamin C and the form most clinical studies use. Ethyl ascorbic acid and other derivatives are more stable but must be partly converted in skin. Protected L-ascorbic acid aims to give the stability of a derivative with the real molecule.

What pH does a vitamin C serum need?

Classic free L-ascorbic acid serums are formulated below about pH 3.5 so the vitamin can enter skin, based on the Pinnell absorption studies. Encapsulated ingredients have their own ranges; SCH ShellCare Vit C, for example, has a pH of 3.5–6.5. Always follow the grade's TDS.

What is encapsulated vitamin C?

It is vitamin C enclosed in a protective carrier, such as a polycaprolactone capsule, gold-particle carrier or porous microsphere, so it is shielded from oxygen and light in the bottle and released on skin.

What does ferulic acid add to vitamin C?

Ferulic acid is an antioxidant that helps stabilise vitamin C and vitamin E. In a published study it stabilised a vitamin C and E solution and doubled its photoprotection of skin. SCH Actives Antiox C combines it with L-ascorbic acid and gold.

Is a vitamin C serum good for glowing skin?

Vitamin C is widely used for brightening and antioxidant care because it reduces melanin formation and neutralises free radicals. Results depend on the finished formula, its stability and regular use with sun protection; claims must be tested on the final product.

What is the use level of SCH Actives Antiox C?

SCH recommends 0.3–0.5% in the finished product, and its efficacy studies use 0.3%. For SCH ShellCare Vit C and the Microsponge grades, please ask us for the current TDS and starting formulas.
